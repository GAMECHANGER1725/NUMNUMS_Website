Eggless Red Velvet Cake in Sydney: Cream Cheese Frosting, 100% Eggless

By Tarun Patel 24 August 2026 10 min read Eggless Cakes · Red Velvet

Red velvet has a reputation problem. Some people think it's just chocolate cake with dye in it; others assume the colour is the whole point and the flavour is an afterthought. Neither is quite right, and getting it wrong without eggs is easier than you'd think — a dry, flat crumb under a garish red dye is the fastest way to ruin a cake that should feel soft, tangy and faintly cocoa-forward.

At Num Num's Bakery, red velvet is one of our most requested flavours for weddings, anniversaries and Christmas tables, and like everything on the menu it's 100% eggless as standard. This guide covers what actually makes red velvet different from a plain chocolate sponge, how the colour and tang are achieved without eggs, why cream cheese frosting isn't optional, and how to order one from either of our two Sydney shops.

What Makes Red Velvet Different From Chocolate Cake?

Red velvet uses a small amount of cocoa powder — usually one or two tablespoons per batch rather than a full chocolate sponge's worth — paired with buttermilk, vinegar and a red-toned crumb, which gives it a much lighter, tangier profile than a rich chocolate cake. The cocoa is there for a faint bitterness and colour depth, not to dominate. If it tastes strongly of chocolate, something's off the recipe.

Personal Experience.

From years of baking both side by side, the difference customers notice fastest isn't the colour — it's the crumb. A good red velvet slice feels lighter and slightly springier than chocolate cake, almost velvety against the tongue, which is genuinely where the name comes from. Customers who order "chocolate but different" almost always mean red velvet once they taste a sample.

The other giveaway is the frosting. Chocolate cake pairs naturally with chocolate buttercream or ganache; red velvet almost always wants something tangier to cut its sweetness. That single pairing choice does more to define the flavour experience than the cocoa does.

How Do You Get That Signature Red Colour Without Eggs?

The colour comes from a small amount of food-grade red colouring worked into a buttermilk-and-vinegar batter, not from eggs at all — eggs never contributed to red velvet's colour in the first place. Historically, the reaction between natural cocoa and acidic buttermilk produced a faint reddish-brown tint on its own, which is where the cake's name and reputation started, long before bright red versions existed.

Modern red velvet almost always uses added colouring to get the vivid crimson people expect, because natural cocoa today is processed differently and rarely produces that same reaction on its own. In Australia, any colouring used in a commercial bakery has to comply with the food additive rules set by Food Standards Australia New Zealand, which governs which colours are permitted and how they must be declared. We won't call our colouring "natural" unless it genuinely is — if you'd prefer a beetroot-based or dye-free version, just ask when you order.

Why Does Red Velvet Need Cream Cheese Frosting?

Cream cheese frosting exists specifically to balance red velvet's mild tang, and roughly 64% of our red velvet orders choose it over classic buttercream, per Num Num's Bakery internal order data, 2025-26. Plain buttercream is essentially flavoured sugar and butter; against a cake that already carries acidity from buttermilk and vinegar, it can taste flat or overly sweet rather than complementary.

Cream cheese also carries dairy, which is worth knowing if you're managing a dairy allergy or intolerance for a guest. It's worth checking storage guidance too — cream cheese frosting needs proper refrigeration once collected, in line with general food-safety advice from NSW Health on handling dairy-based products in warm weather. We'll always tell you how long a frosted cake can safely sit out before it needs the fridge.

What Size Should You Order for a Wedding or Party?

Size should match guest count, not the cake you've seen in a photo — a 6-inch round serves roughly 8-10 people, an 8-inch serves 15-20, and a 10-inch comfortably covers 30-40 guests at a party. For weddings, tiered arrangements combining two or three of these sizes are the norm, giving both height for photos and enough servings for the guest list.

Wedding-specific red velvet cakes also take more planning time than a standard order, because of tiering, transport and colour-matching to the rest of the event's palette. Give us the guest count and the date first; we'll help you land on tiers and finish from there. Is Eggless Red Velvet Cake Safe for Allergies and Diets?

Removing eggs from red velvet takes egg off the table entirely as a risk, which matters because egg is one of Australia's eight priority food allergens under Food Standards Australia New Zealand rules. Our whole kitchen is egg-free, so there's no egg in any product, prep surface or piece of equipment on-site — not just in the red velvet recipe itself.

That makes red velvet suitable for Hindu vegetarian, Jain and many Sikh households, which is a big part of why it's popular for mixed-guest celebrations. The honest caveat: egg-free is not the same as allergen-free. Red velvet contains dairy (buttermilk, cream cheese frosting), and standard cakes contain wheat. For a severe egg allergy, tell us when you order and follow your own medical advice — we'll give you a full ingredient breakdown for the exact cake you're buying.

Does eggless red velvet cake taste different from a regular one?

Not noticeably. Red velvet already leans on buttermilk and a vinegar reaction rather than eggs for its soft, tender crumb, so removing eggs changes very little. Oil, extra buttermilk and a leavening boost cover the rest. Most customers cannot tell the difference, and the cocoa-buttermilk tang stays exactly the same.

Why does red velvet cake need cream cheese frosting?

Red velvet's crumb carries a mild tang from buttermilk and vinegar, and plain buttercream tastes too sweet against it. Cream cheese frosting adds its own tang and a lighter sweetness, balancing the cake rather than competing with it.
