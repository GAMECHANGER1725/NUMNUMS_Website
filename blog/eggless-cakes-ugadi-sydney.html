What Is Ugadi and When Is It Celebrated in Sydney?

Ugadi marks the Telugu and Kannada New Year, opening the new lunisolar calendar year with rituals, an oil bath, and Panchanga Sravanam — the ceremonial reading of the year's almanac. In 2026 the festival falls on 20 March in Australia, marking the start of Sri Parabhava nama Samvatsara. That lands during NSW Term 1, so most families celebrate on the closest weekend rather than the exact date.

The centrepiece of the day is Ugadi Pachadi. This dish combines six tastes — sweet, sour, salty, bitter, tangy and astringent — from ingredients like raw mango, neem flowers, jaggery and tamarind. Each taste represents a different emotion life brings over the coming year. Homes are decorated with mango leaf toranas, and new clothes mark the fresh start.

Telugu is spoken by an estimated 90,400 people in Australia. Kannada speaker numbers grew 51.6% between the 2016 and 2021 Censuses (Australian Bureau of Statistics, Cultural Diversity in Australia, 2021 Census). That growth has been driven largely by skilled migration, concentrated heavily in Sydney's tech and engineering sectors.

What Rituals and Foods Mark an Ugadi Celebration?

Ugadi begins with an oil bath before sunrise, a practice believed to cleanse the body for the year ahead. Families follow this with Panchanga Sravanam. This is the ritual reading of predictions for the coming year from the newly released almanac, usually led by a priest or family elder. Homes are swept, decorated with rangoli patterns at the doorstep, and hung with fresh mango leaf toranas.

Food carries the day's central message. Ugadi Pachadi combines six tastes in one dish: sweetness from jaggery, sourness from raw mango, bitterness from neem flowers, tanginess from tamarind, saltiness, and a mild spice. Eating all six together represents accepting whatever the year brings, good or difficult, rather than wishing for only sweetness.

Are Eggless Cakes Suitable for Ugadi's Vegetarian Traditions?

Egg-free baking aligns closely with the vegetarian practices many Telugu and Kannada Hindu households keep around Ugadi. According to Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ), eggs are one of the eight priority food allergens in Australia. A fully egg-free kitchen removes that cross-contamination risk entirely, not just from the recipe.

At Num Num's Bakery, eggs are not used in any product, any preparation area, or any equipment on-site. The entire kitchen is egg-free, which matters for:

  • Vegetarian Hindu households — many lacto-vegetarian dietary practices exclude eggs entirely, especially around festival days.
  • Egg-allergic guests — a fully egg-free kitchen removes a cross-contamination pathway that a "special order" cake in a mixed kitchen cannot.
  • Jain households — Jain dietary practice excludes eggs; our full menu is compatible on that axis.
  • Vegan or plant-based families — note our cakes still contain dairy (milk powder, butter); we are fully egg-free, not vegan.

If you have allergen or dietary questions beyond eggs, message us directly on WhatsApp before ordering. We'll give you a full ingredient breakdown for your chosen flavour, so there's no guesswork on the day.
