What Is Teej and When Is It Celebrated in 2026?

Teej 2026 is observed on Monday, 14 September, when married women traditionally undertake a nirjala vrat — a strict fast without food or water — praying for the health and longevity of their husbands, while unmarried women pray for a good future partner. The evening before, known as Dar Khane Din, is a joyful feast where extended family gathers to share a large meal before the fast begins the following morning.

Women dress in red saris, sindoor, and bangles reminiscent of bridal attire, apply mehendi, and often visit temples together. The observance closes with Rishi Panchami on 16 September, marking a period of purification. It's a festival built around community, family gatherings, and — particularly on Dar Khane Din — genuinely festive shared food.

Teej is observed by Hindu and Nepali women across South Asia and its diaspora communities, centred on a strict nirjala fast for married women's marital wellbeing, preceded by the Dar Khane Din feast and closed by Rishi Panchami. In 2026 the main fasting day falls on Monday, 14 September.

Why Do Sydney's Nepali and Hindu Communities Celebrate Teej?

Australia's Nepal-born population grew by 123.7% between the 2016 and 2021 ABS Censuses, rising from just over 24,600 in 2011 to more than 122,500 people in 2021 — making it the third-fastest-growing migrant birthplace group in the country after India and China. A large share of that community has arrived since 2015, and Sydney is home to a significant proportion of it.

For a fast-growing, relatively young community — over two-thirds of Nepal-born Australians are aged 25 to 44 — maintaining festivals like Teej away from home matters. Family gatherings, shared feasts, and cultural continuity all take on extra weight when a community is still establishing itself in a new country, and food is often the easiest way to keep that connection alive.

Is Num Num's Bakery Suitable for a Teej Fast Day?

The main day of Teej is typically observed as a nirjala vrat — a complete fast from food and water — so a celebration cake isn't part of that day itself. Where a cake fits naturally is the Dar Khane Din feast the evening before, when the fast hasn't yet started and families gather for a large shared meal, or the celebration once the fast is broken and normal eating resumes.

According to Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ), eggs are one of the eight priority food allergens in Australia, and our kitchen doesn't use eggs in any product, preparation area, or piece of equipment. That matters for lacto-vegetarian Hindu households, where eggs are often excluded as a form of non-vegetarianism, and for Jain households, where our full menu is compatible on the egg axis.

We are not a certified kosher or halal kitchen, and we hold no Monash University low FODMAP certification — our claim is limited to being genuinely, entirely egg-free. If you have specific allergen or dietary questions for your Teej celebration, message us on WhatsApp before ordering and we'll walk through the ingredient list for your chosen flavour.
