That distinction matters more than it sounds. Being egg-free removes one specific cross-contact risk from the kitchen. Is Num Num's Bakery Nut-Free?

No — Num Num's Bakery is 100% egg-free, but it is not a certified nut-free kitchen, and we won't tell you otherwise. Tree nuts and peanuts are two of the priority food allergens that Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) requires food businesses to declare, alongside egg, milk, wheat, soy, sesame, fish, crustacea and lupin. Being egg-free addresses one of those categories. It says nothing about the other ten.

Every Num Num's cake is baked in a kitchen that is 100% free of eggs — a structural claim about the recipe and equipment (FSANZ, Food Allergies, 2026). That claim doesn't extend to nuts, dairy, or wheat, so a nut-allergic customer should never read "eggless" as shorthand for "allergen-free."

We say this because the two claims get confused constantly. Why does that mix-up happen so often? "Eggless" describes what's missing from the batter. "Nut-free" is a facility-wide certification that requires dedicated equipment, air-handling controls, and third-party audits most small bakeries — including ours — don't have. We'd rather be the bakery that's upfront about the gap than the one that gets it wrong when it actually matters.

The Kitchen Is Egg-Free, Not Nut-Free: Here's the Difference.

No eggs are used anywhere in the kitchen, on any equipment, in any product — that's a structural fact we can state with confidence because it's how the entire bakery was built from day one. Nuts are a different story: Ferrero Rocher chocolate and Rasmalai garnish both pass through the same shared kitchen, on shared benches, sometimes on the same day as a nut-free order.

That's not a flaw unique to us — it's the reality of almost every small bakery that isn't a dedicated, audited, allergen-free facility. So why don't more bakeries just say this outright? Certified nut-free status typically requires separate preparation rooms, dedicated utensils and ovens, and regular third-party swab testing. We don't have that setup, and rather than imply otherwise with vague marketing language, we'd rather you know exactly where the line sits.

Here's what that means in practice for a family ordering a birthday cake: if your child's allergy is mild and doctor-managed, ingredient-level information about a nut-free-by-recipe flavour like Chocolate or Vanilla may be all you need. If the allergy is anaphylactic, ingredient information alone isn't a safety guarantee — cross-contact risk in a shared kitchen is real, and it needs to be weighed against your own medical advice, not just our recipe list.

How Common Is Nut Allergy in Australia?

Peanut allergy affects around 3% of Australian infants, and challenge-confirmed tree nut allergy sits at roughly 3.3% by age six, with cashew the single most common culprit at 2.7% (ASCIA, Peanut Allergy — Fast Facts; ASCIA, Tree Nut Allergy — Fast Facts). Among school-aged children and adolescents, nut allergy prevalence climbs to around 4–5% overall, making peanuts and tree nuts among the most common food allergies in Australian classrooms and, by extension, at birthday parties. Every nut allergy is different, so the single most useful thing you can do is name your specific allergen and its severity when you message us — for example, "anaphylactic to cashew and hazelnut" tells us far more than "nut allergy." Why does that extra detail matter so much? What we won't do is promise a guarantee we can't back up with certification.
