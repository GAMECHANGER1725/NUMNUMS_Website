When Is Purim in 2027, and Why Does the Date Change Every Year?

Purim 2027 begins at sundown on Monday, 22 March, and ends at nightfall on Tuesday, 23 March, falling on Adar 14 of the Hebrew calendar (Hebcal.com, Purim 2027). That's a full nine days later than Purim 2026, which fell on 2–3 March, and it lands nine days later still than Purim 2025's 13–14 March observance. There is no single fixed Gregorian date — Purim moves every single year.

The reason is the Hebrew calendar itself, which is lunisolar rather than purely solar like the Gregorian calendar we use for booking cakes. Purim always falls on the 14th day of the Hebrew month of Adar, but Adar's position relative to the Gregorian calendar shifts, and in Hebrew leap years a second month — Adar I — is inserted, pushing Purim further into what would otherwise be a Gregorian February date. That's why the holiday typically lands somewhere between mid-February and late March, never on a date you can simply circle on a standard wall calendar in advance.

Purim always falls on Adar 14 of the Hebrew calendar, but because that calendar is lunisolar and periodically inserts a leap month, the Gregorian date shifts every year — 13 March in 2025, 2 March in 2026, and 22 March in 2027 (Hebcal.com). For Sydney hosts, that means checking the current year's date before locking in any cake order.

What Is Purim, and How Do People Celebrate It?

Purim commemorates the deliverance of the Jewish people in ancient Persia, as told in the Book of Esther, when Queen Esther and her cousin Mordechai exposed and foiled a plot by the royal official Haman to destroy the Jewish population of the empire (My Jewish Learning, "What Is Purim?"). It's one of the most joyful dates on the Jewish calendar, marked by public reading of the Megillah (the scroll of Esther), noise-making whenever Haman's name is read aloud, and a general spirit of costume and celebration rather than solemnity.

Traditional Purim customs extend well beyond the synagogue reading. Mishloach manot — gift baskets of food shared between friends, family, and neighbours — are a central mitzvah of the day. Tzedakah (charitable giving) is another core obligation. And the seudah, a festive meal held during the afternoon of Purim day, is where families and communities gather to eat, drink, and mark the holiday together. Costumes and children's Purim carnivals round out a day that, unlike many Jewish festivals, leans deliberately towards fun.

Unique Insight.

Most festivals we cover lean solemn or reflective — Rosh Hashanah's reflection, Passover's retelling, Hanukkah's quiet nightly ritual. Purim is the outlier: costumes, noise-makers, and a carnival atmosphere are actively encouraged parts of the observance, not incidental fun bolted on. That changes what a complementary cake order tends to look like — brighter colours, playful themes, and less formal styling than a Rosh Hashanah or Passover request.

Is Num Num's Bakery a Certified Kosher Kitchen?

No — Num Num's Bakery is not a certified kosher kitchen, and we want that stated plainly rather than left for a customer to assume. "100% eggless" describes an egg-free kitchen and recipe process; it says nothing about kosher certification, which requires specific rabbinical supervision, ingredient sourcing, and equipment standards that are a completely separate undertaking from removing eggs.

Personal Experience.

We occasionally hear from customers who assume "eggless" must also mean "kosher," and the honest answer is no, it doesn't. Egg-free is a claim about one ingredient category. Kosher certification is a formal process covering the entire kitchen and supply chain. If kosher certification genuinely matters for your Purim seudah — a synagogue event or a strictly observant household — please order from a certified kosher bakery instead of assuming our kitchen qualifies.

Our cakes also contain dairy — milk powder and butter feature across most flavours — and standard cakes contain wheat, so they are not suitable for a strict gluten-free or dairy-free table without discussing substitutions first. Eggs are one of the eight priority allergens regulated under Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) rules, and our kitchen is structurally built around removing that one allergen entirely, not around any broader certification. If you have specific allergen or dietary questions, ask us directly before ordering.
