How to Read Cake Ingredient Labels for Hidden Egg

By Tarun Patel 14 August 2026 11 min read Eggless Cakes · Ingredients

If a cake box says "egg-free" on the front but the ingredient list underneath includes albumin, lysozyme, or a lecithin with no sourcing noted, you're not imagining the contradiction. Egg hides behind dozens of technical and scientific names in packaged food, and even bakery-made items carry allergen statements written in language most home cooks were never taught to decode.

This guide breaks down exactly what to look for on a cake ingredient label. It covers the alternate names for egg, the difference between a deliberate ingredient and a "may contain" warning, and how Australia's food allergen laws actually work. It also explains why, at Num Num's Bakery, we built our entire kitchen around skipping this problem altogether. Every cake we sell is 100% egg-free, so there's no label left to decode.

Quick Summary
  • Egg can appear as albumin, ovalbumin, globulin, lysozyme, livetin, or ambiguous lecithin.
  • FSANZ requires egg — one of 8 priority allergens — declared in plain language.

Why Do Cake Labels Hide Egg Behind Unfamiliar Ingredient Names?

Egg must be declared on Australian food labels. But brands are allowed to use its scientific or technical name instead of the plain word "egg," under the allergen labelling rules set out by Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ). That single rule is why a shopper scanning for the word "egg" can walk straight past it, hidden inside a name like albumin or ovalbumin.

This isn't a loophole so much as a labelling quirk. FSANZ separately mandates a plain-language allergen summary statement — usually a line reading "Contains: egg" — beneath the ingredient list precisely because ingredient names alone are so inconsistent. The catch is that not every product displays that summary line clearly, and unpackaged bakery-counter items sold loose sometimes carry no printed statement at all.

Under Australia's Food Standards Code, egg is one of eight priority allergens manufacturers must declare, either by its common name or via a mandatory allergen summary statement (Food Standards Australia New Zealand). Relying on the ingredient list's technical names alone, without checking that summary line, is the single most common way an egg-avoidant shopper misses it.

Which Ingredient Names Actually Mean Egg Is in the Product?

At least nine distinct ingredient names on an Australian food label can mean egg, and none of them contain the word "egg" itself. Albumin (or albumen), globulin, livetin, lysozyme, ovalbumin, ovomucin, ovomucoid, ovovitellin, and silici albuminate are all egg-derived proteins used for structure, shine, foaming, or preservation in packaged baking.

Lecithin (labelled E322) is the trickiest one on the list. It's usually made from soy but can be sourced from egg yolk, and the ingredient name alone often doesn't say which. FSANZ rules mean a brand must flag egg-derived lecithin in the allergen summary statement. If that line doesn't mention egg, it's worth contacting the brand directly when the stakes are high. What Does "May Contain Traces of Egg" Actually Mean?

"May contain egg" is a precautionary allergen statement covering the risk of accidental cross-contamination during manufacturing. It is not a declaration that egg was deliberately added, and food businesses aren't required to use consistent wording for it (Food Standards Australia New Zealand). For someone avoiding egg by preference, that distinction barely matters. For someone managing a diagnosed egg allergy, it matters enormously, because trace contamination can still trigger a reaction.

These precautionary statements have multiplied across packaged food as shared manufacturing lines have become more common. Allergy & Anaphylaxis Australia advises treating "may contain" wording as a genuine warning rather than boilerplate to skim past, even when the ingredient list itself contains no egg-derived name at all.

In 2025, the National Allergy Centre of Excellence found that 8.2 million Australians — 30% of the population — live with allergic disease, more than double the 4.1 million (19.6%) recorded in 2007 (NACE, Costly Reactions report, August 2025). That growth is a large part of why ingredient-label literacy has become a mainstream household skill rather than a niche one.

How Do You Read a Cake Label in Under a Minute?

A cake label can be checked for egg in four steps that take under a minute once you know where to look. Scan the bolded allergen summary statement first, then the ingredient list for the nine egg-derived names above. Check any "may contain" line next, then ask directly if the item is sold loose from a bakery counter without a printed label at all.

  1. Read the bolded summary line first. Most Australian packaging carries a "Contains: egg" or similar statement in bold beneath the ingredient list — this is the fastest, most reliable check FSANZ requires.
  2. Scan the ingredient list for the nine egg names. Albumin, ovalbumin, globulin, livetin, lysozyme, ovomucin, ovomucoid, ovovitellin, and silici albuminate all mean egg, even without the word "egg" appearing.
  3. Check any "may contain" line separately. Treat it as a genuine cross-contamination warning, not boilerplate, and never assume its absence means the product was tested for egg residue.
  4. Ask directly when there's no printed label. Bakery-counter cakes sold loose, market stalls, and some cafés aren't always required to display a full allergen statement — ask the person serving you before you buy.

Is a Cake Safe for a Severe Egg Allergy Once You've Checked the Label?

Checking a label reduces risk but never fully replaces asking the brand or baker directly, according to ASCIA (Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy) guidance for people managing a diagnosed food allergy. For anyone with a severe or anaphylactic egg allergy, a label check is the first step, not the last one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What ingredient names on a cake label actually mean egg?

Egg can appear under names FSANZ requires manufacturers to declare, including albumin, ovalbumin, globulin, lysozyme, and livetin. Egg lecithin is trickier — lecithin can come from soy or egg, so check the allergen summary statement below the ingredient list rather than judging by the ingredient name alone, since sourcing isn't always spelled out in the name itself.

Does "may contain egg" mean the product definitely has egg in it?

No. "May contain egg" is a precautionary statement covering accidental cross-contamination during manufacturing, not a deliberate ingredient, and businesses aren't required to use consistent wording for it. For someone avoiding egg by preference that distinction barely matters, but for a diagnosed egg allergy it matters enormously, since trace contamination can still trigger a reaction.
