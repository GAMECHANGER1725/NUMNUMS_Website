What Is Chuseok, and Why Does It Matter to Sydney's Korean Community?

Chuseok is a three-day harvest festival held on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month. Families in Korea travel home, share a feast, and perform charye, a table-setting ancestral rite offering rice, fruit, and songpyeon to departed relatives. It is one of the two biggest holidays on the Korean calendar, alongside Lunar New Year. Greater Sydney was home to 50,702 people born in South Korea at the 2021 Census. That figure comes from the Australian Bureau of Statistics 2021 Census QuickStats for Greater Sydney. It's roughly half of the 102,096 Korea-born residents counted nationwide.

Most of that community sits in a well-known corridor: Eastwood, Epping, Carlingford, and Strathfield, all suburbs Num Num's Bakery already bakes for regularly. Chuseok in Sydney rarely involves a public holiday or a day off work, so gatherings often shift to the nearest weekend. That flexibility is part of what makes a cake ordered ahead of time so useful. The date can move, but the cake still needs to be ready.

Unique Insight.

Cross-referencing the two ABS QuickStats pages shows almost exactly half of Australia's Korean-born population lives in Greater Sydney. That's 50,702 of 102,096 people, or 49.7%. That's a heavier metro concentration than several other festival communities we've written about. It helps explain why Chuseok-adjacent orders cluster so tightly around a handful of north-west and inner-west suburbs.

When Is Chuseok in 2026, and How Do Sydney Families Mark the Holiday?

Chuseok 2026 runs from Thursday 24 September to Saturday 26 September, with the festival day itself falling on the middle day, 25 September. In Korea, that means a public holiday and mass domestic travel. In Sydney, there's no public holiday attached, so most Korean-Australian families move their gathering to whichever weekend sits closest to the date instead.

A typical Sydney Chuseok gathering brings together three or four generations for a shared meal. It's usually held at the home of the eldest family member. Songpyeon-making is often a group activity, with grandparents teaching grandchildren how to shape and steam the rice cakes. Games, family photos, and a relaxed afternoon of eating follow the more formal parts of the day.

What a Modern Sydney Chuseok Table Often Includes.

  • Songpyeon — half-moon rice cakes filled with sesame, chestnut, or sweet bean, made at home or bought from a Korean grocer.
  • Japchae and jeon — stir-fried glass noodles and pan-fried savoury pancakes, staples at almost every Korean gathering.
  • Fresh fruit — Korean pear and persimmon, both traditionally part of a harvest table.
  • A shared celebration cake — a common addition for the younger generation, often chosen because it's egg-free and easy for everyone to eat.

Does Num Num's Bakery Make Traditional Korean Rice Cakes for Chuseok?

No, and we want to be direct about that. Num Num's Bakery does not make songpyeon or any other traditional Korean tteok. We do not attempt to replicate charye-sang, the ancestral-rite food offering set out during Chuseok. We are a 100% eggless Western-style cake bakery. Our cakes are not a substitute for songpyeon and were never designed to be one.

Personal Experience.

We've had a handful of customers ask, quite reasonably, whether we could make a cake shaped like a songpyeon for Chuseok. We're honest with them: we don't have the rice-flour technique or steaming equipment that traditional tteok requires. Attempting a decorative imitation would risk trivialising something that carries real ancestral significance for the family ordering it. What we offer instead is a genuine, well-made eggless cake for the parts of the day built around modern celebration.

Under the Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) framework, eggs sit alongside milk, tree nuts, and six other substances as a priority allergen. That's the standard our kitchen is built around: no egg is stored, prepared, or handled on-site, in any recipe. It says nothing about kosher, halal, or any other certification. It also says nothing about traditional Korean baking methods — it's simply the allergen framework behind our own eggless claim.
