What Is a Rasmalai Cake, and Why Is It One of Sydney's Most-Requested Eggless Flavours?

From Num Num's internal order data across both Sydney shops, Rasmalai is the second most-ordered eggless flavour after Chocolate, and the single most-requested flavour for Diwali and wedding cakes. It's a 100% eggless sponge built around saffron, cardamom and milk-sweet notes, named after the Bengali dessert it draws its flavour from.

Rasmalai as a dessert — soft paneer dumplings soaked in sweetened, saffron-infused milk — has no cake precedent in Western baking. Translating that flavour profile into a sponge that holds up without eggs took real recipe work, which is exactly why it's rare to find outside a dedicated eggless kitchen. See the full range of 15 eggless flavours or browse photos on the Our Cakes page.

The name itself describes the dish: "ras" means juice or syrup, and "malai" means cream. Rasmalai traces back to Bengal and Odisha, where paneer dumplings are simmered in sugar syrup before soaking in reduced, saffron-scented milk. That origin matters for the cake version too — the flavour target isn't a generic "Indian dessert" note, it's a specific balance of milk sweetness, saffron and cardamom that a rushed one-off substitution rarely gets right.

A kitchen that has never used eggs at all has no fallback recipe to lean on, so every flavour, Rasmalai included, has to work on its own terms.

How Does Num Num's Make Its Rasmalai Cake 100% Eggless?

[Personal Experience]

From our years of recipe development across both Sydney kitchens, replicating rasmalai's milk-sweet richness without eggs took several rounds of testing. Eggs are traditionally used in a Western sponge for structure and moisture — an eggless version needs a different binding approach entirely, and Rasmalai's delicate saffron-milk profile made that harder than most of our other flavours.

Every cake starts from the same egg-free base used across the whole menu. The Rasmalai variant is then built with a cardamom-saffron syrup soak and a milk-forward cream, finished with a light almond and pistachio garnish — the same finishing touch you'd see on the traditional dessert. Nothing about the process involves eggs at any stage, in any preparation area, or on any equipment.

Texture is the other variable that has to hold up without eggs. A traditional Western sponge relies on egg proteins to trap air during baking; without them, an eggless batter needs the right balance of raising agent, fat and liquid to stay light rather than dense. Getting that balance right for a milk-heavy flavour like Rasmalai — where the syrup soak itself adds extra moisture — took noticeably more testing than a drier flavour like Chocolate or Vanilla.

That single-purpose focus is why we can commit to a flavour like Rasmalai at all. See how the recipe compares with the wider Indian-inspired range on our Eggless Indian Cake Flavours guide.

How Does Rasmalai Cake Compare to Traditional Rasmalai Dessert?

Traditional rasmalai is soft paneer dumplings served cold, soaked in sweetened, saffron-infused milk — a milk-based dessert, not a baked one. Num Num's Rasmalai cake keeps the same flavour signature (saffron, cardamom, milk-sweet richness, almond-pistachio garnish) but delivers it as a sliceable sponge cake, built for a celebration table rather than a bowl.

Rasmalai cake is genuinely egg-free, but it is a dairy-forward flavour by design — milk powder, cream and a milk-sweet soak are central to getting the taste right, so it is not suitable for a dairy allergy.

Are Rasmalai Cakes Suitable for Vegetarian, Jain, and Allergy-Conscious Guests?

[Original Data]

From our internal order data, Rasmalai cake orders skew heavily towards Hindu vegetarian, Jain and Sikh households — customers regularly cite the flavour by name because it's the closest cake equivalent to a mithai they already trust. Being able to hand every guest a slice, regardless of dietary background, is consistently the reason given for choosing it over a general bakery's cake.

Many Hindu vegetarian and Jain dietary traditions exclude eggs as non-vegetarian, so a genuinely egg-free Rasmalai cake fits without any substitution or special request. It also suits Sikh households following similar dietary codes. That said, honesty matters here: Rasmalai cake is not vegan or dairy-free (it contains milk powder, butter and cream), and the standard garnish includes almond and pistachio, so it is not nut-free either.

Num Num's is not a certified kosher, halal or FODMAP-friendly kitchen — the 100% eggless claim covers eggs specifically, not every dietary framework. If you have a nut or dairy allergy, or need a specific certification, tell us when you order via WhatsApp so we can talk through what the kitchen can and can't guarantee before you commit.
