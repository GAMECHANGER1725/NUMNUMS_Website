Eggless Cake and Keto Diets: Is It Ever Low-Carb?

By Tarun Patel 16 August 2026 9 min read Dietary · Eggless Cakes

"Is your eggless cake keto?" is a question we hear from Sydney customers more often than you might expect, usually from someone managing type 2 diabetes, following a strict low-carb plan, or just trying to be polite about a birthday cake they know they shouldn't finish. The honest answer is no, and we'd rather tell you that plainly than let "eggless" sound like something it isn't.

This guide exists because the two words get confused constantly. Eggless describes what's missing from the recipe: eggs. Keto and low-carb describe something completely different: how many grams of carbohydrate end up on the plate. Removing eggs changes neither the flour nor the sugar in a cake, which are the two ingredients that actually matter for a keto or low-carb reader.

A ketogenic diet typically limits total carbohydrate intake to just 20-50 grams a day, according to healthdirect Australia, the government-funded health information service. A standard slice of iced celebration cake can carry roughly 45-55 grams on its own. For a keto reader, that single slice can use up an entire day's carbohydrate budget in one sitting.

Is Eggless Cake Keto or Low-Carb?

No, eggless cake is not keto or low-carb by default. Being egg-free addresses one specific thing: the eight priority allergens tracked under Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) labelling rules, of which egg is one. Carbohydrate content sits in a completely separate category, governed by flour, sugar and portion size, none of which change when eggs are removed from a recipe.

We understand why the two get mixed up. "Eggless" sounds like a health modifier, the same family of word as "sugar-free" or "gluten-free." It isn't. It's an allergen and dietary-practice distinction, built for Hindu vegetarian households, Sikh dietary practice, and egg allergy sufferers. None of those groups are necessarily managing carbohydrate intake, and a keto or low-carb reader isn't necessarily avoiding eggs. The two audiences overlap by coincidence sometimes, never by recipe design.

A ketogenic diet typically restricts total carbohydrate intake to just 20-50 grams per day, according to healthdirect Australia. Because standard cake relies on wheat flour and sugar for its structure and sweetness, removing eggs alone cannot bring a slice anywhere near that daily limit.

Why Isn't Traditional Cake Keto-Friendly?

Traditional cake is not keto-friendly because its two dominant ingredients by weight, wheat flour and sugar, are both concentrated carbohydrate sources. Wheat flour is close to 75% carbohydrate by weight, and refined sugar is effectively 100% carbohydrate, so a sponge built from those two ingredients starts high in carbs before icing, filling, or fruit are even added.

Personal Experience.

We've had customers assume "eggless" and "keto" were interchangeable simply because both sound like dietary restriction categories. When we explain the actual ingredient list, most understand quickly; a few have been visibly disappointed, which is exactly why we'd rather have this conversation in writing, upfront, than leave anyone guessing at a party.

The chemistry matters here too. A cake's rise, structure and moisture come from gluten development in the flour and moisture retention from sugar, a process that genuine keto baking replaces entirely with almond flour, coconut flour, and sugar alcohols like erythritol. That's not a small tweak to an existing recipe; it's a different recipe built from different ingredients with different baking behaviour altogether.

Under NSW Government healthy eating guidance, cake sits in the "discretionary food" category alongside other high-sugar, high-refined-carb treats, meant to be eaten occasionally rather than as a daily staple. That classification exists regardless of whether the recipe contains eggs. It's a useful, honest way to frame any celebration cake, ours included.

Does Num Num's Bakery Offer Keto or Low-Carb Cakes?

No, we do not currently offer a dedicated keto or low-carb product line, and we want that stated plainly rather than discovered after an order. Our entire menu is built on standard wheat flour, dairy, and cane sugar, the same base ingredients used across most Sydney celebration bakeries. Being 100% eggless changes none of that.

Every cake we make also contains dairy, in the form of milk powder, butter or cream, and some flavours involve nuts, so egg-free never means allergen-free beyond eggs. If you're managing a severe or anaphylactic allergy alongside a low-carb diet, state your full requirements when ordering and follow your own medical guidance, aligned with ASCIA and Allergy & Anaphylaxis Australia advice, since we cannot promise medical-grade safety.

Unique Insight.

The keto question and the allergy question actually pull in opposite directions ingredient-wise. Genuine keto baking swaps out wheat flour for nut-based flours, which introduces a new allergen risk for a nut-allergic customer. There's no single cake formula that solves keto, egg-free and nut-free at once — a real trade-off worth knowing before you ask a bakery for all three.

We're not a Monash-certified low FODMAP kitchen, and in the same spirit, we're not a keto-certified or low-carb-certified kitchen either. No certification exists that we hold and aren't telling you about. If a strict keto or low-carb formulation is what you genuinely need, we'd rather point you toward a bakery that specialises in it than sell you something that doesn't fit the brief.

How Many Carbs Are in a Standard Slice of Eggless Cake?

A standard fully-iced slice of Num Num's celebration cake carries approximately 45-55 grams of carbohydrate, based on our own internal recipe estimate — not a laboratory-tested figure, but a consistent, honest range across our standard flavours. That's broadly in line with commercial cake nutrition data published under FSANZ's AUSNUT food composition program, which analysed cakes and slices for starch, sugar and other nutrients across the Australian market.

Break that down and roughly two-thirds comes from the sponge itself — the flour, sugar and any fruit puree in the batter — with the remaining third coming from icing and filling. A thicker buttercream or ganache layer pushes the icing share higher again, since sugar is the dominant ingredient in almost every icing style we make.

For readers who also count total calories rather than just carbs, our separate eggless cake calories guide and full nutrition breakdown cover energy, sugar and protein content slice-by-slice in more depth than carbohydrate alone.

What Can Keto or Low-Carb Guests Do at a Celebration?

From our order conversations across both shops, the most realistic strategy isn't avoiding the cake table entirely — it's controlling the portion. A thin sliver of standard cake, roughly a third of a normal serve, brings the carbohydrate load down closer to 15-20 grams, which is a meaningfully different number to plan around than a full 50-gram slice.

Original Data.

From requests we've fielded across Harris Park and Riverstone, customers managing carb intake almost never ask us to reformulate the recipe. They ask for a smaller cake overall, or specifically request thin slices be cut at serving time. That's a request any bakery, including ours, can genuinely accommodate.

Being sociable at a celebration doesn't have to mean eating a full slice or nothing at all. A small taste keeps a keto or low-carb guest part of the moment — cutting the cake, singing, taking a photo — without derailing a day's carb budget the way a full serve would. That's a workable compromise most people managing a strict diet have already made peace with long before they walk into a party.

Which Flavours Work Best for a Smaller, Shared Serving?

Our lower-sugar options guide already covers which flavours need less added sugar per full slice — Mango, Lychee and Strawberry lead that list because ripe fruit carries its own natural sweetness. That guide answers a different question to this one: it's about reducing sugar density across a normal-sized serve, not about carbohydrate totals for someone strictly counting grams.

For a keto or low-carb guest specifically, the flavour matters less than the icing ratio and the slice size, because flour is the bigger carbohydrate contributor either way. That said, a lighter buttercream flavour with less filling volume, like Vanilla or Chocolate with reduced icing, trims a few extra grams compared to a dense, multi-layer Ferrero Rocher or Red Velvet build.
