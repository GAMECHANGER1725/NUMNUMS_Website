What Is Sinhala & Tamil New Year, and Why Does It Matter in Sydney?

Aluth Avurudda (Sinhala) and Puthandu (Tamil) mark the sun's astrological move from Pisces to Aries, historically tied to the harvest season. The date is fixed by the solar calendar, usually 13 or 14 April, and the festival is unusual in that Sinhalese and Tamil communities in Sri Lanka have traditionally celebrated it together, across ethnic and religious lines.

In Sydney, that shared spirit carries over. Sinhalese Buddhist and Tamil Hindu families in Western Sydney often mark the day with near-identical rituals: lighting the hearth at an auspicious time, preparing kiribath (milk rice), visiting relatives, and giving small gifts to children. A cake has become a natural modern addition once the traditional dishes are served, especially for larger multi-generational gatherings.

In 2021, NSW recorded 31,427 Sri Lanka-born residents, a 9% rise from 2016, with 52% of that population concentrated in the Western Sydney Local Health District (ABS Census QuickStats, 2021). That concentration means Western Sydney bakeries see genuine, recurring demand around the mid-April new year period.

What Traditions Mark Aluth Avurudda and Puthandu?

The festival opens with a "nonagathe" (neutral period) between the old and new year, when families avoid work and instead pray, rest or play traditional games. It ends with the lighting of the hearth at an astrologically fixed auspicious time, when kiribath (milk rice) is cooked first in most households.

After the hearth-lighting comes house visits — younger relatives visit elders first, exchange gifts, and receive blessings. Sweets play a central role throughout: kiribath, kokis (a crisp fried treat), and mango-based dishes for Tamil households celebrating Puthandu. A shared cake at the end of visiting hours has become common in Sydney, particularly for families hosting extended relatives who no longer live nearby.

How Is Sinhala & Tamil New Year Different From Diwali or Lunar New Year?

Aluth Avurudda and Puthandu are fixed to the solar calendar, always landing on 13 or 14 April, unlike Diwali or Lunar New Year, which shift each year against the Western calendar (our Diwali guide covers those movable dates in more detail). That fixed date makes catering easier to plan around for bakeries, but it also means demand clusters into a narrow one-week window rather than spreading across a season.

The other difference is scope. Diwali and Lunar New Year each centre on one dominant community. Aluth Avurudda and Puthandu are genuinely shared: Sinhalese Buddhists and Tamil Hindus in the same Sydney suburb, sometimes the same extended family through intermarriage, mark the day together. A bakery serving both groups from one egg-free kitchen fits that shared celebration better than a menu split by community.

Are Eggless Cakes Suitable for Sri Lankan and Tamil Dietary Practices?

Eggs are one of the eight priority food allergens in Australia under strict labelling law, according to Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ). Cross-contamination from a bakery that also handles eggs elsewhere on-site is a genuine risk for allergic families, not a theoretical one.

At Num Num's Bakery, eggs are never used in any product, prep area, or piece of equipment on-site. That's a kitchen-wide policy, not a recipe substitution. It matters for:

  • Hindu Tamil households — many Tamil Hindu dietary traditions exclude eggs as a form of lacto-vegetarian practice, especially around religious observances.
  • Buddhist Sinhalese households — some Sri Lankan Buddhist families follow vegetarian or near-vegetarian diets, particularly on religious or festival days.
  • Mixed extended families — New Year gatherings often bring together relatives with different dietary rules under one roof; a fully egg-free cake removes the need for a separate "safe" dessert.
  • Egg-allergic guests — with allergic disease now affecting 30% of Australians (NACE, Costly Reactions report, 2025), a shared cake with no allergy caveats is genuinely useful at a large family event.
  • Vegan or plant-based guests — note our cakes contain dairy (milk powder, butter); we are not a vegan bakery, but we are fully egg-free.

If you have allergen questions beyond eggs, message us directly on WhatsApp before ordering and we'll give you a full ingredient breakdown for your chosen flavour. You can also browse the Indian Sweets range for additional festival gifting options.
