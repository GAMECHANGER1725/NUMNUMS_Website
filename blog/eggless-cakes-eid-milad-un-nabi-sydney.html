What Is Eid Milad-un-Nabi and How Is It Different from the Two Eids?

Eid Milad-un-Nabi, also written Mawlid al-Nabi or simply Milad, commemorates the birth of the Prophet Muhammad on 12 Rabi' al-Awwal, the third month of the Islamic lunar calendar. Because the Islamic calendar is lunar, the Gregorian date shifts each year — in 2026, it falls on Tuesday, 25 August.

It is entirely separate from Eid al-Fitr, which marks the end of Ramadan, and Eid al-Adha, which falls during the Hajj pilgrimage season. Observance of Mawlid also varies significantly between different schools of Islamic thought. Many Sunni communities, particularly those with South Asian, Middle Eastern, and North African heritage, mark it with gatherings, recitations, and shared meals. Other traditions, including some Salafi and Wahhabi interpretations, do not observe it at all, according to The Conversation's analysis of global Mawlid practice.

Why Do Sydney Families Order a Cake for a Milad Gathering?

In many South Asian and Middle Eastern Muslim households, a Milad gathering closes with a cake cut and shared among guests, alongside sweets and a communal meal. A special cake is often baked specifically to mark the occasion, according to community accounts of Mawlid observance collected by Yalla World. It's a custom rather than a religious requirement, and it varies from family to family and community to community.

For Sydney households hosting a Milad at home, the gathering usually brings together neighbours, extended family, and members of the local mosque community. It's often a genuinely mixed guest list where dietary needs vary widely from table to table.

How Do Sydney's Muslim Communities Mark the Day Differently?

Sydney's Pakistani, Bangladeshi, and North Indian communities often mark Mawlid with a Jashn-e-Eid Milad-un-Nabi gathering. It's typically a home or hall event built around naat recitation (devotional poetry praising the Prophet), a shared meal, and the closing cake. Turkish and Southeast Asian communities tend to favour a quieter mosque-centred observance, with fewer large home receptions.

Lebanese, Egyptian, and other Middle Eastern Sunni communities in Sydney observe the day with varying degrees of formality, ranging from a family dinner to a larger community mawlid at the local mosque. Australia's Muslim population draws from more than 60 countries of birth, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics. No single format describes how the day is marked across the city, and that's part of what makes catering for it interesting.

What stays consistent across these different styles is the closing cake. Whether the gathering is a formal mosque function or a small family dinner, a shared dessert at the end signals the celebration has reached its natural close. That's exactly the moment our customers usually message us to place their order — often planning a full year ahead once a family decides a cake belongs in the tradition.

Why Choose an Eggless Cake for a Mawlid Celebration?

In 2025, 8.2 million Australians — roughly 30% of the population — lived with allergic disease, nearly double the 4.1 million recorded in 2007, according to the National Allergy Centre of Excellence's "Costly Reactions" report. A Milad guest list that spans a mosque committee, extended family, and neighbours will almost always include someone managing a dietary restriction.

[Unique Insight]

Halal-certified eggs are permitted in Islamic dietary law, so egg-free isn't a religious requirement for Mawlid the way it is for some Hindu or Jain observances. What we hear most often instead is a practical concern: hosts don't want to run a second "special" cake for allergic guests when one all-eggless cake can serve everyone at the table.

Our kitchen never uses eggs in any product or preparation area, which removes egg cross-contamination entirely. However, our cakes do contain dairy — milk powder and butter — and may involve nuts, and standard cakes contain wheat; we aren't an allergen-free bakery. According to Food Standards Australia New Zealand, eggs are one of the eight priority food allergens requiring clear labelling in Australia. For severe or anaphylactic egg allergy, tell us your needs when ordering and follow your own ASCIA-aligned medical advice — see Allergy & Anaphylaxis Australia for guidance.
