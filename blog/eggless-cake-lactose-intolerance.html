Eggless Cake for Lactose Intolerance: What We Can (and Can't) Promise

By Tarun Patel 28 July 2026 10 min read Dietary · Eggless Cakes

If you're lactose intolerant and love the idea of an eggless cake, here's the honest answer up front: egg-free is not the same as dairy-free. Num Num's kitchen is 100% egg-free — every one of our 15 flavours, no exceptions — but our standard recipes still use milk powder, butter and cream. That means our cakes are not automatically safe for lactose intolerance, and we'd rather tell you that plainly than let you find out the hard way.

This guide explains what lactose intolerance actually is, why removing eggs does nothing to remove dairy, and what practical, honest options exist if you still want a slice at your next celebration. We'll also cover what to tell us when you place an order, so nobody is surprised at pickup.

What's the Difference Between Lactose Intolerance and a Dairy Allergy?

Lactose intolerance is a digestive sensitivity, not an immune-system allergy — the small intestine doesn't make enough of the enzyme lactase, so milk sugar passes through undigested and causes bloating, gas or discomfort. A dairy (cow's milk) allergy is a completely different mechanism: the immune system reacts to milk proteins, and reactions can range from mild to severe. Confusing the two matters, because the safe response to each is different.

According to the Victorian Government's Better Health Channel, a form of lactose intolerance that develops after early childhood is common among people of Asian, Middle Eastern, Mediterranean and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander heritage, while it stays rare among people of Northern European descent. That's a genetic and cultural pattern, not a food safety failure on anyone's part — it simply means a large share of Sydney's multicultural community lives with some degree of lactose sensitivity.

Is Num Num's Eggless Cake Safe for Lactose Intolerance?

Not automatically — our cakes are 100% egg-free, but every standard flavour still contains dairy, including milk powder, butter and cream, so nobody with a lactose sensitivity should assume "eggless" means "safe for me." Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) requires milk to be clearly declared on packaged food labels under the Plain English Allergen Labelling rules, precisely because milk-based ingredients aren't always obvious from a product's name or appearance.

We say this clearly because it's the whole point of this guide: being a dedicated eggless bakery removes the egg cross-contamination pathway from our kitchen, but it doesn't touch the dairy content of the recipe at all. If your lactose sensitivity is severe, please treat our standard range as unsuitable and speak to your own doctor or dietitian before ordering anything from us.

Why Doesn't Egg-Free Mean Dairy-Free?

Because eggs and dairy do completely different jobs in a cake recipe, so removing one has no bearing on the other. Eggs bind and lift the batter; when we take eggs out, we replace that structural role with dairy-friendly ingredients like yoghurt, condensed milk or buttermilk — which, if anything, can add more dairy, not less. An eggless cake is not a "lighter" or "cleaner" cake by default; it's simply an egg-free one.

From our years of taking orders across both Sydney shops, this is the single biggest misunderstanding customers bring to us. Many assume that because we're a specialist eggless bakery, our cakes must be free of most common allergens generally. They're not — we make one specific promise (no eggs, ever) and we keep it completely, but that promise was never meant to cover dairy, gluten or nuts as well.

This is also why reading an ingredients list still matters, even at a bakery that specialises in one exclusion. For a related but separate question, see our guide on the difference between dairy-free and eggless cakes, which covers the milk-allergy side of this same confusion in more depth.

How Common Is Lactose Intolerance in Sydney's Communities?

Precise national figures are hard to pin down because many cases go undiagnosed, but the pattern is well established: lactose intolerance becomes common in adulthood among people of Asian, Middle Eastern, Mediterranean and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander background, while it remains rare among people of Northern European heritage (Better Health Channel). Sydney's western suburbs, where both our shops sit, are among the most culturally diverse in the country — so this isn't a niche question for us, it comes up often.

The commercial market reflects the same trend. Lactose-free dairy products in Australia are estimated at roughly AUD 650–750 million in 2026, forecast to grow toward AUD 1.3–1.6 billion by 2035 as more households manage some form of lactose sensitivity at home. That's a genuine, measurable shift in how Australians shop for dairy — even though it says nothing about any individual bakery's recipes.

Can Someone With Lactose Intolerance Still Enjoy a Small Slice?

For many people with mild lactose intolerance, a small portion of cake is manageable, according to healthdirect Australia, which notes that most people with lactose intolerance can tolerate small amounts of milk-based food and don't need to cut dairy out completely. Some customers also choose to take an over-the-counter lactase enzyme supplement before eating, which can help the body process the lactose in a slice of cake.

From the WhatsApp conversations we have every week, the customers who manage best are the ones who plan ahead — they order a smaller slice, save it for after a meal rather than on an empty stomach, or take their usual lactase tablet beforehand. What we can't do is tell you, individually, whether a slice is safe for your body. That's a decision for you and your own doctor or dietitian, especially if your symptoms have ever been severe. We can only be transparent about ingredients so you can make that call with full information — ask us for a full ingredient breakdown any time.

Does This Affect Our Indian Sweets Too?

Yes — the same dairy caveat applies across our whole kitchen, not just cakes. Many traditional Indian sweets on our Indian Sweets menu use ghee, milk solids (khoya) or condensed milk as core ingredients, so a lactose-sensitive guest should ask about specific sweets the same way they'd ask about a cake. Being egg-free is a bakery-wide promise here; being lactose-friendly isn't, in cakes or sweets.

One thing we've noticed over years of mixed-family orders: it's rarely the birthday person who has the dietary concern, it's often a grandparent or a visiting relative. Isn't it usually the case that the person managing everyone else's plate is the last to mention their own restriction? Asking us directly, even briefly, tends to solve it faster than guessing on the day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Num Num's eggless cake safe for lactose intolerance?

Not automatically. Our cakes are 100% egg-free but they still contain dairy — milk powder, butter and cream — in every standard flavour. Some customers with mild lactose intolerance manage a small slice fine, but that depends entirely on their own tolerance. We can't promise a dairy cake is lactose-safe, so please tell us your needs before ordering.

What's the difference between lactose intolerance and a dairy allergy?

Lactose intolerance is a digestive issue — the body lacks enough of the enzyme lactase to break down milk sugar, causing bloating or discomfort. A dairy allergy is an immune reaction to milk proteins and can be far more serious. They're different conditions with different risks, so treat any dairy allergy as strictly off our standard menu.

Can I still order a Num Num's cake if I'm lactose intolerant?

Many customers with mild lactose intolerance do, often in smaller portions or after taking a lactase enzyme supplement beforehand. Does egg-free mean less dairy than a regular cake?

No. Removing eggs doesn't reduce dairy content — our recipes replace egg's binding role with milk-based ingredients like yoghurt or condensed milk, so an eggless cake can contain a similar or even slightly higher proportion of dairy than a standard cake. Being egg-free says nothing about how much lactose is inside.
