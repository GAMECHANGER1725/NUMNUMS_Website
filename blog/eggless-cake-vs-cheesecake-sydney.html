Eggless Cake vs Cheesecake: Does It Actually Have Eggs?

By Tarun Patel 9 August 2026 11 min read Eggless Cakes · Dietary

Cheesecake and eggless cake solve two different problems on a Sydney dessert table, and the egg question is exactly where they diverge. Most baked, New York-style cheesecake is built around eggs — they set the cream-cheese filling into that dense, sliceable block as it bakes. Eggless cake, by design, never touches an egg at any stage of production.

At Num Num's Bakery we hear this question constantly from customers planning a birthday, engagement, or office party who assume "cheesecake" is automatically an egg-free option. It usually isn't. This guide breaks down exactly which cheesecake styles contain eggs, which don't, and why an eggless cake is the more predictable choice whenever eggs need to be off the table entirely.

Does Cheesecake Have Eggs?

Yes, most baked cheesecake contains eggs — typically 2 to 4 whole eggs per standard 9-inch filling, added specifically to set the cream-cheese mixture as it bakes. This is different to a regular cake recipe, where eggs mainly add moisture and lift. In a baked cheesecake, egg proteins are the primary setting agent, doing a job closer to what gelatine does in a jelly than what eggs do in a sponge.

Not every cheesecake follows this rule, though, which is exactly where confusion sets in for Sydney shoppers. Baked, oven-set cheesecake — the New York style most cafes and bakeries serve — relies on eggs almost every time. No-bake cheesecake, set in the fridge rather than the oven, is a different animal and usually skips eggs entirely. We'll cover that distinction properly further down.

Baked cheesecake typically uses 2-4 eggs per filling because egg proteins coagulate under heat, firming the cream-cheese mixture into a sliceable set. Eggless cake achieves its structure through chemical leaveners instead, meaning it never depends on eggs at any point in the recipe or the bake.

What's the Real Texture and Flavour Difference Between Eggless Cake and Cheesecake?

The two desserts feel completely different in the mouth, and it comes down to what each recipe is built from. From our years serving both cake lovers and cheesecake fans across Harris Park and Riverstone, eggless cake has a soft, slightly springy crumb, while cheesecake is dense, creamy, and closer to a baked custard than a traditional cake at all.

Cheesecake's flavour profile leans tangy and rich, driven by cream cheese and often balanced with a buttery biscuit base. Eggless cake's flavour range is far broader — from light fruit flavours like Strawberry and Mango through to indulgent options like Ferrero Rocher and Chocolate — because cake batter accepts flavouring far more flexibly than a set custard filling does.

Why Do Eggs Matter So Much in a Baked Cheesecake?

From how conventional cheesecake recipes are built, eggs are the structural backbone, not a flavour ingredient. As the filling bakes, egg proteins unravel and bond together in a process called coagulation, trapping the cream cheese, sugar, and cream in a firm, sliceable matrix. Without that protein network, a baked cheesecake filling would stay loose and never set properly.

[Unique Insight]

This is the reverse of how eggless cake works. A cake's structure comes from gluten development plus chemical leaveners, so removing eggs mostly changes moisture and tenderness. A baked cheesecake's structure comes almost entirely from egg coagulation, so removing eggs means rebuilding the whole setting mechanism from scratch, not just adjusting a recipe slightly.

That's also why a baked cheesecake can crack on top or sink in the middle if it's over-baked or cooled too quickly — the egg proteins have overset or contracted unevenly. Eggless cake doesn't share that failure mode at all, since its structure comes from a completely different chemical process that isn't tied to egg protein behaviour in the same fragile way.

Is No-Bake Cheesecake Egg-Free?

Usually, yes — no-bake cheesecake is typically egg-free by nature, since it sets in the fridge using gelatine, whipped cream, or a condensed-milk mixture rather than the baked egg protein coagulation a traditional New York-style cheesecake relies on. This is the single most important nuance in this whole comparison, and it's the part most people miss.

Not every cheesecake has eggs — only most baked, oven-set versions do. A minority of mousse-style no-bake recipes still fold in raw or lightly cooked egg for extra creaminess, so the safest approach is always to ask rather than assume from the name "no-bake" alone. The Food Standards Australia New Zealand egg food safety guidance is a useful reference for how raw or lightly cooked egg dishes should be handled safely if a recipe does include them.

No-bake cheesecake is typically egg-free, relying on gelatine or whipped cream to set rather than baked egg protein. Baked, New York-style cheesecake is the version most likely to contain eggs, since egg coagulation under oven heat is its primary setting mechanism. The two styles should never be assumed identical on egg content.

Can You Make a Baked Cheesecake Without Eggs?

Yes, eggless baked cheesecake recipes exist, but they need a substitute thickener — typically cornstarch or arrowroot — to compensate for the missing egg proteins that would otherwise set the filling. The trade-off is real: an eggless baked cheesecake generally rises less and turns out less tall than a traditional egg-based version, and can be slightly softer in the final set.

Why Does This Matter for Egg Allergies and Dietary Restrictions in Sydney?

Egg allergy affects approximately 9% of Australian infants, making it one of the most common childhood food allergies, according to Allergy & Anaphylaxis Australia. For families managing that allergy, ordering "cheesecake" from a mainstream cafe or bakery menu without knowing which style it is can be a genuine risk, since a baked version very likely contains the exact ingredient being avoided.

Eggs sit among the priority food allergens tracked under Australia's food labelling rules, per Food Standards Australia New Zealand, alongside milk, peanuts, and tree nuts. This matters just as much for Hindu vegetarian and Sikh households avoiding eggs for religious reasons, and for anyone catering a mixed guest list where some attendees eat eggs and others don't.
