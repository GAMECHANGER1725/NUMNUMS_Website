What Is Bhai Dooj, and When Does It Fall in 2026?

Bhai Dooj 2026 falls on Tuesday, 10 November, the second day of the bright fortnight of the Hindu month of Kartik. It closes the five-day Diwali festival period, coming two days after the main Diwali night. Because the date follows the lunar calendar, the Gregorian date shifts a little each year, usually landing in late October or November.

The festival goes by several names depending on region. In Maharashtra and Goa it's called Bhau-Beej. Elsewhere it's known as Yama Dwitiya, referencing the mythological reunion of Yamaraj, the god of death, with his sister Yamuna. Across every regional name, the core ritual stays the same: a sister applies tilak to her brother's forehead, ties a protective thread or offers sweets, and prays for his long life. In return, brothers offer gifts and a promise of protection.

How Large Is Sydney's Hindu Community, and How Fast Is It Growing?

Hinduism is one of Australia's fastest-growing religions. The 2021 ABS Census recorded 684,002 Hindus nationally, a 55.3% increase from 440,300 in 2016. More than 210,500 recent migrants who arrived between 2016 and 2021 identified as Hindu, and over 91% of them were born in India, Nepal or Sri Lanka. Hindus now make up 2.7% of the national population, up sharply from a decade earlier.

Western Sydney, close to both of our shops, holds a large share of that growth. What Happens on Bhai Dooj Day?

Bhai Dooj begins with the tilak ceremony, usually performed at home in the late morning or early afternoon after checking an auspicious muhurat, or timing window, for the year. The sister applies a red tilak paste to her brother's forehead, sometimes with rice grains pressed into it, and performs an aarti with a small lamp. She may tie a protective thread around his wrist, similar in spirit to Raksha Bandhan, and offers sweets by hand.

The brother responds with a gift, traditionally cash or jewellery, and a promise to look out for his sister. Afterwards, families gather for a meal, and this is where a cake most often appears in Sydney households — as a shared dessert once the formal ritual is done, alongside barfi, laddoo and other Diwali-season sweets already on the table.

For families with siblings living apart, the day can involve visiting a brother's or sister's home, or hosting them for the occasion. In Sydney, where extended family often lives across several suburbs rather than in one household, Bhai Dooj sometimes becomes the excuse for the whole family to gather in one place for the first time since Diwali night itself. A shared cake works well for exactly that kind of larger, multi-generational gathering.

Are Eggless Cakes Suitable for Every Family at the Bhai Dooj Table?

Egg-free baking aligns with several dietary frameworks common at a Bhai Dooj gathering. According to Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ), egg is one of the eight priority food allergens in Australia, with strict labelling rules — and for egg-allergic guests, cross-contamination from a bakery that also handles egg-based batters elsewhere is a real risk.

At Num Num's Bakery, eggs are never used in any product, preparation area or piece of equipment on-site. That distinction matters for the full mix of guests a Bhai Dooj gathering usually brings together, including:

  • Hindu vegetarian households — many Hindu dietary traditions exclude eggs entirely, especially during festival observances.
  • Jain guests — Jain dietary practices exclude eggs; our entire menu is Jain-compatible on the egg axis.
  • Egg-allergic family members — a fully egg-free kitchen removes cross-contamination risk from other egg-based batters entirely.
  • Vegan or plant-based guests — note our cakes contain dairy (milk powder, butter); we're not a vegan bakery, but every cake is fully egg-free.

If you have specific allergen questions beyond eggs, contact us directly via WhatsApp before ordering, and we'll give you a complete ingredient breakdown for your chosen flavour.

Because Bhai Dooj gatherings often mix generations — grandparents keeping strict traditional diets alongside younger relatives with no particular restriction — having one cake that genuinely works for everyone removes the need to negotiate a separate dessert for anyone at the table.

This also matters for mixed-faith households, which are increasingly common across Sydney's western suburbs. When a Hindu family hosts in-laws or friends from a different background, an eggless cake sidesteps a common source of last-minute stress: nobody has to ask quietly whether the dessert is suitable before serving it, and nobody feels singled out for needing a different slice.
